Why San Antonio Couples Call Me

A Note on Bilingual Weddings in San Antonio

A lot of San Antonio couples reach out specifically because they've struggled to find a wedding band that handles both English and Spanish well. Most don't. Most wedding bands in Texas have a couple of "crossover" Spanish songs they pull out as a token gesture, and you can hear the difference immediately — the phrasing is off, the pronunciation is shaky, the connection to the room when those songs hit isn't there.

That's not how I work. I grew up performing in Spanish as much as English. Boleros, salsa, bachata, contemporary Latin pop, Tejano standards — these are part of my core repertoire, not a side dish. When I play "Bésame Mucho" at a San Antonio wedding, it's not a novelty song. It's the song that makes the abuela cry and the cousins get up.

If your wedding is bilingual, multicultural, or just has one set of grandparents who'd love to hear music in their language, that's not an upcharge with me. It's the default.

The Framework

How I Think About Weddings

Most wedding-band websites are sales pitches. I'd rather give you the actual framework I'd use if I were helping a friend think this through.

Here's what I've come to believe after more than a decade of doing this: the music isn't the point of a wedding, but it's the thing that holds everything else together. Done well, nobody talks about it — they talk about how the night felt. Done poorly, it's the only thing they remember. The food, the flowers, the venue all blur together a year later. The dance floor doesn't.

What I try to do at every wedding I play is read what the room actually needs in the moment, not what the timeline says it needs. When the toast is running long, I play softer. When the abuela starts crying during the parent dance, I let the song breathe. When energy on the dance floor dips, I shift the next three songs without anyone noticing the adjustment.

That kind of presence — being a real musician with you, not a playlist for you — is most of what I think you're hiring when you hire someone like me. The rest of this page is the framework I'd walk a friend through before they booked anyone. Whether or not it's me.

The Five Questions

How to Choose the Right Wedding Band

Whether or not you end up hiring me, here are the questions that actually determine what you need.

1

Will you have music during the ceremony, or just the reception?

If yes, you need an instrument that works acoustically — piano, classical guitar, or strings — and a musician who can handle the precision of cued moments (processional, recessional, ring exchange). For Catholic ceremonies, which are still common in San Antonio, there's often specific liturgical music required, and the church may have its own organist or musician requirements. Worth asking your celebrant.

If your ceremony is at the venue rather than a church, solo piano is the most flexible option — handles processional and recessional beautifully, and transitions easily into cocktail hour.

2

Live music the whole night, or live early and transition to dancing later?

Some couples want continuous live performance from cocktail hour to last call. Others want live music for the emotional moments — ceremony, first dance, parent dances — and then a DJ for high-energy late-night dancing.

For multicultural San Antonio weddings, the "live early, DJ later" model often works well, because a DJ can handle genre transitions (cumbia, Tejano, current pop, ranchera) more efficiently than a band rotating through repertoire. But if you want the full live-band moment for the dance floor, that's also doable.

3

Do you need bilingual or Spanish-language music?

Yes, I do. Bilingual sets, Spanish-language sets, Tejano repertoire, Latin standards — all of it. No upcharge. Bring me your specific song requests at the consultation and I'll tell you honestly which ones I already know, which ones I can learn before your date, and which ones (if any) wouldn't be a good fit.

This is the question San Antonio couples ask most, and it bears repeating: I didn't grow up with Spanish as a second language. It's where I started.

4

Which instruments are non-negotiable?

Here's the honest hierarchy:

  • —Bare minimum for a "feels live" experience: Lead vocals, piano, guitar. A skilled musician can layer in the drum and bass parts live. This is the solo or duo configuration.
  • —The next priority is a live drummer. Most couples notice the energy shift the moment a real kit is in the room.
  • —Then add a bass player. Now you have a real rhythm section, and the dance floor lights up differently.
  • —From there, the floor opens up. For San Antonio weddings leaning into Latin repertoire, brass and percussion go a long way. Go as big as your venue and budget support.
5

What's your budget, and what does that get you?

Wedding music budgets in San Antonio tend to run more conservatively than Austin or Dallas — the market supports a wider range, and you can put together excellent live music for less than you might expect. The range is anywhere from a few hundred dollars to $8,000+ for a full band with an extended set.

Most couples I talk to a year after their wedding regret cutting this category more than any other. The food and flowers blur. The dance floor doesn't. For specifics on what your budget supports, that's a consultation conversation.

Configuration

Solo, Trio, or Full Band?

Most couples assume they have to pick one. I offer something in between — the same vendor across the entire night, scaling up and down as the moment requires.

Ceremony

Solo piano, often with vocals — including bilingual selections. Handles processional and recessional with precision, whether in a church, chapel, or outdoor garden setting.

Cocktail Hour

Solo piano with layered accompaniment, or piano plus acoustic guitar — often with a Latin guitar flavor for SA weddings. Elegant without overwhelming conversation.

Dinner

Solo piano with vocals, or a stripped-back trio. Bilingual set flows naturally here — boleros and standards during dinner, contemporary pop building toward the reception.

Reception & Dance Floor

You're not stuck with one configuration. You're hiring one person who knows your event and your music, and who shows up with whichever lineup we agreed on.

Know Your Venue

San Antonio Wedding Venues

San Antonio has some of the most distinctive wedding venues in Texas — and the venue character should shape the music as much as the acoustics do.

Historic & Mission-Style

Pearl Stable, Hotel Emma, the McNay Art Museum — character that pairs beautifully with bilingual repertoire and Latin guitar. These rooms reward musicians who know what to do with them.

Downtown Hotels

La Cantera, the JW Marriott, Hyatt Regency — larger scale, often 200+ guests, designed for events. Clean sound regardless of size. Full-band configurations work well here.

Hill Country & Ranch Venues

Properties toward Boerne, Spring Branch, and the wine country north of town. Outdoor ceremonies, often more relaxed timelines. Solo or trio configurations tend to suit these best.

Tell Me Your Venue

If you've booked yours, tell me at the consultation. San Antonio's venue landscape is genuinely varied — the right configuration depends on the room, not just the headcount.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you do Tejano, cumbia, or other Latin genres beyond Spanish-language pop?

Yes, with a caveat: some sub-genres I know well (boleros, salsa, bachata, contemporary Latin pop) and some I do at a working level rather than expert (full Tejano repertoire, regional Mexican). Tell me your specific must-play songs at the consultation and I'll be honest about what I'd nail and what might be better handled by a specialist or DJ for those tracks.

Can you handle a Catholic ceremony?

Often, yes — depending on the parish and the celebrant's specific requirements. Some San Antonio churches require their own musicians or have liturgical guidelines that restrict outside performers. I work with whatever the celebrant approves, and I'm happy to coordinate directly with the church if needed.

How does travel from Austin work for a San Antonio wedding?

San Antonio is barely a travel commitment from Austin — 80 miles, roughly 90 minutes door-to-door. For most San Antonio weddings, travel is included or nominally itemized. I don't typically need overnight lodging unless the schedule starts unusually early or runs late enough that the drive back doesn't make sense.

How far in advance should we book?

San Antonio's peak season runs March–May and September–November, but the city has more year-round wedding activity than Austin or Dallas. Saturday dates in peak months book 6–9 months out. Off-peak or weekday is more flexible. Full-band configurations need more lead time regardless of season.

Can you put together a full band with San Antonio-based musicians?

Yes. I work regularly with SA-based players, and for full-band configurations I often build the band with a mix of Austin and San Antonio musicians depending on availability and instrumentation. Local players for the rhythm section often make logistical and financial sense.

What does pricing depend on?

Number of musicians, performance hours, travel (minimal for SA), specialty equipment, and additional services. Pricing is provided in writing after the consultation. There are no hidden fees, and the bilingual repertoire is included — not a line item.

Can you learn songs that aren't in your catalogue?

Often, yes — in any language. Songs in keys and chord progressions adjacent to my existing repertoire are easiest to add. Bring your "must-play" list to the consultation and I'll tell you honestly what's feasible before your date.
